## Overview

Scope is Incentiva's custom-built app to run the field teams it operates for Nequi and Wompi. Its field staff spend the working day in the street, logging merchant visits from their phones, and not all of those phones run Android. The app was on Google Play, but it had not shipped on iOS yet, and moving the app's Firebase project, which carries the push notifications, into the company's ownership was part of the job.

I took over release management with two goals: get the app onto the App Store, and turn releasing from an event into a routine. A field app that is hard to update is a field app whose bugs stay in the street.

**Result: the app's first iOS release, then 13 production versions across the App Store and Google Play.**

**What the business gained:** Field staff on iPhones can now work in the app, and Incentiva owns what it needs to keep shipping it. When a coordinator reports a problem from the field, the answer is a version and a date.

## The Build

### Making iOS build at all

The first work was the unglamorous kind. iOS signing and builds had to work reliably, from a clean checkout, every time. Background GPS had to work too, because visit tracking depends on it, and on iOS that meant a native location monitor that keeps reporting while the app is not on screen, within the limits Apple allows. None of it shows in a demo, and all of it decides whether a field worker with an iPhone can start the working day.

### Owning the app's infrastructure

Firebase moved to a company-owned project, for the app and for the API. Users never see a change like that, and a company cannot skip it: whoever owns the project owns the app's notifications, its keys and its future releases. After the move, nothing about shipping Scope depended on a third party.

### Three rejections, three fixes

App Store review rejected the submission three times. Each rejection was read for what the reviewer actually needed, answered and resolved, and the app was approved for unlisted distribution: it is installed by the field team from a direct link, not found by the public in a search. Submissions went through the App Store Connect API rather than by hand, so every round could be repeated exactly.

### Releasing as a routine

Thirteen production versions only work if a release has no manual steps left to forget:

- **Scripted uploads** through the App Store Connect and Google Play APIs, so both stores receive the same build the same way.
- **A mandatory-update mechanism**, so nobody keeps working in the field on a version with a known defect.
- **A version-by-version publication guide**, so every release follows the same checklist and the next person can run it.
- **Launch messages and a first-day guide** for field staff, so a change in the app arrives with an explanation instead of a surprise.

### Ready for the next iOS

When a new iOS version changed how apps start, the app moved to Apple's UIScene lifecycle, the structure the system now expects, rather than being patched around the change, so the next iOS update finds it ready.

The engineering was done with AI coding agents (Claude Code) under my direction. I owned the release decisions, handled App Store review and submitted every version to both stores myself.

## Results

- The app's **first iOS release**, approved for unlisted distribution after **three App Store review rejections** were resolved.
- **13 versions** released to production on both stores.
- Firebase now lives in a company-owned project, and every release follows a scripted, documented path.

## What This Demonstrates

Release management is where product work meets the people using it. A fix can be ready and still not reach the field if signing breaks, a review stalls or uploads depend on one person's laptop. Most of this project was removing those delays: owning the infrastructure, answering the reviewer precisely, and scripting the path to both stores until shipping became ordinary.

The numbers are the proof of that routine. Thirteen production versions are not a sprint; they are a release path that no longer needs heroics.

It also changes what the operation can be promised. When a coordinator reports a problem from the field, the answer is no longer "in the next release, whenever that is", but a version and a date.
